18. って = は?? Mysteries explained! -おうとする, とする, として, という, っていう

18. って = は?? Mysteries explained! -おうとする, とする, として, という, っていう

Lesson 18: ってtte = はwa?? Mysteries explained! Toshite, toiu, to suru, ou to suru, tteiu

こんにちは。

Today we're going to talk about "trying to do something"

and from there we're going to broaden out into the wider meanings of the "と" quotation particle because this is a very central part of Japanese that's used all the time.

So we need to get a firm understanding of what it is and how it works.

Now, last week (Lesson 17) we learned the volitional helper う and よう which makes a word end with the sound "おう" or "よう" and expresses will.

【図】Conjugation chart: the red う-column (う く す つ ぬ ぶ む る) is joined by green arrows to the green お-column (お こ そ と の ぼ も ろ), showing the う→お shift. Right-side examples give かう→かお, あるく→あるこ, はなす→はなそ, もつ→もと, しぬ→しの, とぶ→とぼ, のむ→のも, とる→とろ; a bottom note reads 「Ichidan (“ru-verb”): always just delete the る」 linking たべる→たべ.

If we're "trying" to do something we use the volitional for this.

So if we say, "山にのぼろうとする", this means "try to climb mountain".

Why does it mean that? What's this construction actually doing?

【図】Slide with snowy Mount Fuji, pink cherry blossoms and a small green bird. Text 山にのぼろうとする with のぼる=climb and note “に rather than を is normal here because we are climbing to the target of the summit.” No diagram labels or arrows.

Well, "のぼろう" expresses the will to climb.

【図】Slide on しようとする: a back figure holds a purple staff ending in う while a front pink-haired figure stands near よう, with the pink note "ability not implied." Green hollow arrows link "volition (will)" to う/よう and "action" to する, joined by a green + showing う/よう + する → しようとする.

If we say "山にのぼろう", we're saying, "Let's climb the mountain".

Literally, set our will toward climbing the mountain.

おうとする / とする

"のぼろうとする" means doing the act implied by setting our will to climb the mountain.

If we just wanted to say "climb the mountain", we'd just say, "山にのぼる".

But we're not saying "climb the mountain", we're saying "try to climb the mountain".

Therefore, do the action implied in setting our will / enact our will to climb the mountain,

whether we succeed in actually climbing it or not.

Some people find the distinction between "try climbing" and "try to climb" confusing.

And that's really only because of the way it's expressed in English.

【図】Left-to-right train-car grammar diagram with several cars/engines (one with a clock-like face), a faded carriage, a large directional arrow with lettered endpoints, and Japanese particle/word labels paired with English glosses. A red oval and red notes surround the last two cars. It illustrates Japanese word order and particle linking, especially the auxiliary 〜てみる (te-form + miru, "try doing something").

In Japanese, as we learned recently (Lesson 16), if we want to say,

"try climbing the mountain", we say, "山にのぼってみる."

The difference is that "try climbing / try eating / try swimming" doesn't imply any doubt

about the fact that we can actually do it.

It implies doubt about what would be the result when we've done it.

---

"Try eating" - we know we can eat, but don't know if we'll like it.

"Try eating" - "食べてみる" - means "eat and see".

Eat it and then see what the result is, see if you like it, see if you don't like it.

---

"山にのぼってみる" means "climb the mountain and see".

See whether it was hard, see what the view's like from the top.

---

"ケーキを食べようとする" - "try to eat the cake" - implies that we don't know whether

you can in fact eat the cake or not, but try it anyway.

Maybe it's a huge cake and it would be very hard to eat it all.

So "してみる" - "do and see" - implies that there's no doubt about the fact that we can do it, but there is some doubt about what the result of having done it is going to be.

Are we going to like it?

Is the building going to fall down?

We don't know what will happen when we've done it, but we know we can do it.

"しようとする" implies that we don't know whether we can do it or not,

but we are going to try to do it.

---

So, an important thing here is to see what the と-particle is doing.

-と is encapsulating the sentence that came before it:

"山にのぼろう" - will to climb the mountain.

It isn't quoting it.

It's not something we've said; it's not something we've thought, exactly.

The point is that it's taking the essence, the meaning, the import of that "山にのぼろう"

and putting it into action.

And we're going to find that in other cases.

【図】Top text ホッとする colour-coded ホッ blue, と red, する green, with right-side labels Enact (green), what is (grey), expressed (red), in the (grey), "sound effect" (blue). A chibi grey-haired girl with glasses and a satchel stands at left with a small curl from her mouth to "expressed"; it maps ホッ to sound effect, と to expressed, する to Act.

For example, we may read that someone "ホッとした" . Now, what does that mean?

"ホッ" is in fact a sound effect.

It's the sound effect of breathing a sigh of relief: "ホッ".

But "ホッとする" actually does not mean "breathe a sigh of relief".

What it means is, "feel relief / be relieved".

---

So what we're doing here is enacting the idea, the feeling, expressed in "ホッ", the sigh of relief.

Just as in "山にのぼろうとする" we're enacting the feeling,

the import of setting our will toward climbing the mountain, that is, trying to climb it.

とする & にする

Now, similarly, if we say "さくらを日本人とする", it means regarding Sakura as a Japanese person.

【図】Train-car diagram of 〜を〜とする: a faded が-car glossed “we,” then を (Sakura), と (にほんじん/日本人, “Japanese person”) and する (“do”). A green note says と replaces で here because the sense is “supposing” rather than “being”; glosses read “We Sakura ‘Japanese person’ enact” then blue “We assume/take Sakura to be Japanese” — “(act/think according to the proposition ‘she is Japanese’).”

Now, we might also say, "さくらを日本人にする", but that means something quite different.

It means "turn Sakura into a Japanese person".

に is the target of an action.

---

A little while ago we had a lesson (Lesson 8) in which we talked about

"さくらがかえるになる" - "Sakura becomes a frog".

【図】Slide contrasts ある ('be') with なる ('be-come'), then diagrams さくらは with flag は, white car が labelled Øが ('she'), black car かえるに ('frog-to') and locomotive なった ('became'). A grey arrow points from the topic into the train and a CORE arc links かえるに to なった; below, an arrow runs from a kneeling girl with a ball to a frog with a target.

Now, we've also talked about (Lesson 15) the way that "ある" and "する" are the Eve and Adam of Japanese verbs,

【図】A yin-yang-like circle pairs ある on the white side with する on the black side, below them “BE” and “DO”. A blonde girl at left is labelled ある “Mother of self-move” and a dark-haired boy at right する “Father of other-move”; no arrows, only positional alignment.

"ある" being the primary self-move verb and "する" the primary other-move verb.

"なる" is very closely related to "ある" - "ある" is "be", "なる" is "become".

And so if we say "になる" it means to become something.

If we say "にする" that's the other-move version of "になる".

It means to "turn something into something".

So if we say, "まじょがさくらをカエルにした" - "the witch turned Sakura into a frog".

【図】Particle-train mnemonic: A over the first car, following cars containing が, を, に and B over the locomotive with う; captions まじょが (witch), さくらを (Sakura), カエルに (into a frog), した (did). An arrow runs from さくらを to カエルに with a red target on に; below, a blue zigzag arrow links the witch to kneeling Sakura and a gray arrow links Sakura to the frog.

Note: カエル is just another way to write frog, via katakana. Animals are usually written that way.
"さくらを日本人にする" - turning Sakura into a Japanese person;

but "さくらを日本人とする" - regarding Sakura as a Japanese person / taking Sakura as a Japanese person.

【図】Train-style slide for 「さくらを 日本人と する のが」: four cars with side kana が, を, と, う above fragments のが, さくらを, 日本人と, する, with A and B over the first and last cars. Colour glosses below give “we” under のが, “Sakura” under さくらを, 日本人と “Japanese person”, する “do/enact”, and blue paraphrases “take Sakura to be Japanese” and “(subscribing to the proposition "she is Japanese")”. A tilted green note reads “と in place of だ here because ‘supposing’ rather than ‘being’ is taking place.”

"かばんをまくらとする."

【図】Glossy brown handbag linked by small thought bubbles to a large cloud containing three pillows, with big opening and closing quotation marks around the pillow cloud. Japanese at upper left: かばんを まくらと する ('use a bag as a pillow'); no arrows.

"かばん" is "bag", "まくら" is "pillow" and this means "using your bag as a pillow".

You're not turning your bag into a pillow, it's not becoming a pillow,

but you're regarding it as one and using it as one.

So here we have some of the uses of "-とする".

Generally speaking, it relates to how we regard something.

として

Now, if we say "-として", this isn't so much the act of regarding something as something,

but seeing something in the light of being something.

So, in English it would usually be translated simply as "as" or “for”. Or “being in the role of”

【図】Light-blue-haired anime girl in a yellow sailor top and red skirt poses above a large smiling green frog in front of a gold star shield labelled 'FROG JOCKEY' / 'PRESIDENT'. Japanese フロッグジョッキー部 / の部長として, glossed 'As president of the Frog Jockey Society'.

So, "こじん/個人としての いけん/意見" means "my opinion as a private person", as opposed to, say, “my opinion as president of the Frog Jockey Society.” - just another example sentence (contrast in words “private person” & “president”), not some difference in the function of として. The wording may delude to Dolly meaning it as if it was として that had an opposing role there.

Or we could say, "アメリカ人として小さい" - "She's small for an American / As an American, she's small.

So we can see that the quotation function of -と is used not only to quote ideas and thoughts, but also to take the feeling of something and bundle it up and then say something about it.

【図】A dark-mode YouTube comment screenshot by "Organic Japanese with Cure Dolly" (3 years ago, edited), not a slide. It argues とする is a completed action/verb ("take/regard as") while として is the connective て-form meaning "as/for/in the role of", with example アメリカ人として小さい.

Note: just in case, this comment may help some in regards to とする & として.

という / と言う

Of course, the most basic thing that can follow -と is "いう",

in which case it's a literal quotation,

-という/と言う (it's usually pronounced not so much "-という" but as "-とゆ").

And this again can be used not just in a literal quotation but also saying

how something is said or what it's called.

So, "ふしぎの国のアリスという本", means "book called ふしぎの国のアリス"

【図】A tilted book cover ふしぎの国のアリス with ●キャロル ●テニエル, ●中山知子, フォア文庫, an Alice-and-rabbit illustration and a cat at the top. Green label to the right: "という usually written in kana when it precedes (modifies) anything," with large pink という本 below. No arrows; proximity links cover to labels.

っていう / って言う

And the -と in "-という" can be reduced simply to -って.

So we can say "-っていう" - "ふしぎの国のアリスっていう本",

or it can be reduced down to just -って.

【図】A pink cartoon face with whiskers and shaded cheeks above three centred lines of pink outlined kana stacked as という / っていう / って, illustrating the colloquial contraction. No arrows.

"ふしぎの国のアリスって本" still means "The book called Alice in Wonderland".

So people sometimes get a little confused when they just see this -って.

It means -と or -という, but the thing that really confuses people sometimes is that

it can also be used in place of the は-particle.

って used in place of は

Now, this seems particularly strange, until you realize what it's actually doing.

If we remember what the は-particle is, the は-particle is the topic-marking particle.

So when we say "さくらは(zeroが)日本人だ", we can put that into English as

"Speaking of Sakura, (she) is Japanese person".

Now, does that start to make things a little clearer?

【図】Two train diagrams: top has flag は with さくらは (“Topic: Sakura”), car A marked が/のが (“she”), and locomotive B marked だ/日本人だ (“Japanese person-is”). Bottom repeats the train after flag って with さくらって (“Sakura-say (ie make-topic)”), car A が/のが, and locomotive B 日本人だ; at lower left are a red-X’d green frog and a girl holding Japan’s flag. No arrows; A/B labels sit over car and locomotive.

"さくらって(zeroが)日本人だ" - "Sakura-say, (she) Japanese-person is" -

"Sakura-speaking of, (she) Japanese-person is" - "Sakura (topic), (she) Japanese-person is".

Now, we can't say -と or -という in place of the は-particle.

It's a very casual use.

We just use -って.

But you can see that it's really, even though it's very colloquial,

it's not just some random and inexplicable thing.

It's setting up Sakura as the thing we're talking about, just as は is.

Now, there are other extended uses of -と.

We'll cover those as we come to them.
